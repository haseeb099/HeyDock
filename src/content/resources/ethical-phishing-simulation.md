## If the organisation has not authorised it, it is not a test

A phishing or social-engineering simulation can teach people to notice and report a suspicious message. It can also damage trust if it is cruel, secret from leadership, or used as a disciplinary trap. An ethical exercise has a written purpose, a responsible owner, a list of things that will not be done, and a coaching plan.

Start with the purpose. 'We want more reports and fewer silent clicks' is a purpose. 'We want to catch people out' is not.

Get authorisation from someone who can permit the use of staff time and internal channels. Involve HR or staff representatives if that is how your organisation normally treats people-related exercises. Do not test customers, suppliers or the public.

Ban cruel pretexts. Family, health, immigration, and other pressure have no place in a learning exercise. Stick to workplace-like messages, and keep the tone proportionate.

Give people a reporting route before the exercise starts, so the right behaviour is possible. Afterward, coach calmly. Share themes with leadership, not a humiliation table. Do not pass a 'gotcha pack' to managers for punishment.
